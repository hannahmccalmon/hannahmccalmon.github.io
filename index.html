About

I am a PhD candidate in the Molecules, Cells and Organisms program at Harvard, working with Venkatesh Murthy. My dissertation, supported by an NIH/NIDCD F31 fellowship, asks how stable odor representations are in the olfactory tubercle — a region sitting at the junction of olfaction and reward, where sensory input and learned value are difficult to disentangle.

Before Harvard I studied biology at Wake Forest, where my honors thesis traced the development of chemoreception in earthworms, and spent an NSF REU at the University of Washington building an anatomical atlas of octopus neural tissue. The through-line has been chemosensation in whatever animal will tolerate the question.

Outside of imaging rigs, I build the analysis tooling my experiments need — a registration pipeline for following the same cells across weeks, decoders for reading odor identity out of population activity, and a low-cost electronic-nose sensor array for characterizing what the animal is actually being given.

Research

Stability of odor representations in the olfactory tubercle

Two-photon calcium imaging of the same neurons across weeks of go/no-go odor learning, asking whether representations drift, and whether introducing a new odor disrupts the ones already learned.

Odor discrimination in complex mixtures

Sparse input representations account for how mice discriminate odors in crowded, concentration-varying mixtures. bioRxiv 2026

Earlier work

Chemoreceptive development in earthworms, and a micro-CT anatomical atlas of octopus neural tissue.
