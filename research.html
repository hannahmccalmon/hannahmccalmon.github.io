Research

I work on chemosensation — how nervous systems represent odors, and how those representations hold up over time and in clutter.

Stability of odor representations in the olfactory tubercle

Harvard University · Murthy Lab · NIH/NIDCD F31 (1F31DC023435)

The olfactory tubercle sits where odor input meets reward, which makes it a useful and awkward place to ask what an odor representation is: activity there reflects both the smell and what the smell has come to mean. My dissertation follows the same identified neurons across weeks of two-photon calcium imaging while mice learn a go/no-go odor discrimination, so that changes in the population code can be measured rather than inferred across animals.

Two questions drive the work. First, how much of the representation is stable once behavior has plateaued — does the code keep drifting after learning stops? Second, what happens when a well-learned olfactory world gains a new odor: does the new odor take up its own place in the population, or does it reshape the representations of odors the animal already knew?

Odor discrimination in complex mixtures

Preprint · bioRxiv, 2026

Natural odors almost never arrive alone, and their concentrations vary from sniff to sniff. This work shows that sparse input representations are enough to account for how mice discriminate target odors within complex, concentration-varying mixtures.
